Context

A class project that started with one bean bag.

Say “dark pattern” to me and the first app I think of is Temu.

This was Project 1 for Dark Patterns, a class where we spent weeks learning to spot manipulative design, and, honestly, how to use it. The brief: take a real product and redesign it the ethical way. So I opened Temu.

I only wanted one thing: a $51.14 bean bag chair. Then a $20 coupon popped up, and I tapped it out of curiosity. A prize wheel. A “$1,000+” reward. A countdown to upgrade it. Five free gifts with no prices. A 20-day check-in. Three “Wait, don’t leave!” screens.

Fifteen minutes later, checkout said $151.34, about three times what I came to spend. I still wasn’t sure what was in my cart.

No single screen lied much. Together, they made leaving hard.

Problem statement

How might we keep the coupon promotion while letting shoppers see the price and leave anytime?

The patterns

Seven tricks, one frame.

The frame · Obstruction

Each screen misleads a little. Stacked, leaving keeps getting harder.

Where · Fig. 12–13, 19–21The reward turns into a 20-day check-in, and every exit is intercepted three times.

Sneaking

Key rules appear only after you’ve invested time.

Fig. 6, 9, 11, 15–17Faded small print, gifts with no price, an unfilled “$0,000+” template.

Interface interference

Bright buttons forward, grey links out.

Why it worksThe path Temu wants is the only one that looks clickable.

False urgency

Countdowns on rewards that never expire.

Why it worksA 04:59 timer leaves no time to read the rules.

Fake social proof

“Others are claiming right now.”

Why it worksUnverifiable activity makes the offer feel scarce.

Preselection

Gifts come pre-picked.

Why it worksRemoving items takes effort, so most people keep them.

Fix 1 · Obstruction

The item comes first.

The coupon is an optional note. Leaving is always one tap. This phone is my live prototype: it tours itself, and you can tap it.

Fix 2 · Sneaking

Price visible from step 1.

Same offer. The difference is when you see the price.

Fix 3 · The 20-day check-in

Keep the check-in. Make it a choice.

Daily check-ins are a fair way to bring people back. The trap was hiding one, pre-checked, inside the coupon. So I kept it, off by default and separate from the order.

Off by defaultIts own page, never switched on silently.
20 days · 4 coupons · $17The whole deal, readable at a glance.
Miss a day, it pausesProgress never resets. The original wiped it.
Leave in one tapNo retention screen. Coupons you earned stay yours.

Ask for notifications in context, then keep the promise.

iOS asks for permission only once, so an in-app sheet explains first: one a day, your chosen time, no ads.

Trade-offs

Honesty costs a little. Trust pays it back.

Short term
  • Fewer impulse add-ons.
  • Some refunds once people see what they bought.
Long term
  • Shoppers return on purpose.
  • Fewer surprise packages and returns.
  • Less risk under FTC click-to-cancel and the EU DSA.
Reflection

Seven patterns, three steps, one chair.

Obstruction is a sumNo screen is a deal-breaker alone. Mapping the whole flow showed the trap.
Be fair to the businessKeeping the promotion made the redesign shippable.
Paper is enough to argueLogic first, pixels second.
What’s next · testing it

Is the redesign actually better? 15–20 people try both flows with one task: buy only the bean bag chair.

I compare completion, taps, what they paid, whether they knew what they paid, and trust. Half see Temu first, half see mine first.
